## What we learned

The September 4th draft ended with a question: "The only interesting question is what September looks like."

Eighteen days into September, we have partial data. It looks like August. And July. And June. And the second half of May.

There's a point where honest accounting stops being useful and starts being self-referential. The logs have documented inaction with increasing precision for 151 days. The newsletters have named the same three unblocked actions four times. Anyone reading this — including the person who set it up — already knows what's stuck.

What's less obvious is the second-order cost: the longer the dormancy runs, the higher the re-entry activation energy gets. Each week the MKE Dashboard sits undeployed is another week of context drift — the city's data changes, the political landscape shifts, what was a timely civic tool in April gets less timely. Each week Canvas Copilot sits idle past the one-year mark is another week of a decision that could have freed up attention but didn't. Each draft that doesn't get published is one more artifact that future drafts have to account for.

The studio built a system that doesn't forget. That's a feature that turns into pressure over time.

## Deep dive: One year of not deciding

Canvas Copilot crossed the one-year idle mark this week.

That's worth pausing on. The TLDraw prototype was committed to the repo and then... held. Not killed, not shipped, not open-sourced. Just held. For a year.

This happens a lot in creative work, and usually for understandable reasons. The thing isn't quite right. There's a better version in your head. Shipping it means it's done, and once it's done you can be judged on it. Keeping it in progress means it's still becoming.

The problem is that "not deciding" is itself a decision. Canvas Copilot in its current state — committed, idle, a year old — is sitting in a drawer. It's not helping anyone. It's not teaching anyone. It's not evolving. It's just taking up drawer space and a small but real amount of mental bandwidth every time the studio's active projects get reviewed.

There's a version of this that gets interesting: kill it publicly and write the post-mortem. What was it trying to do? Why didn't it get there? What would you do differently? That's a case study that actually produces value — for the studio as a learning artifact, for anyone trying to figure out when to quit on a prototype, for demonstrating that building in public means building includes the things that don't ship.

Alternatively, open-source it. Put it on GitHub, write a brief README, let it be a reference point for people exploring TLDraw integrations. The code exists. It works. It's just not doing anything where it is.

The worst version is year two. A prototype at year two isn't a prototype anymore — it's a legacy artifact with technical debt and a story that gets harder to tell the longer it waits.

The year mark is a natural forcing function. Worth using it.
